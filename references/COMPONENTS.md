# COMPONENTS.md — the working-vessel parts catalogue

A reference dataset of **every object that plausibly exists on a working vessel**, written as a
**kit**, not a model set.

Owner direction, 2026-08-09: *"do not tailor these boats, make parts that people can build custom
ships from."* So every row here is the **general form** of a thing — "cleat, 0.25–0.9 units, scales
with line size" — never "the cleat off that Norwegian trawler". If a row only makes sense on one
vessel type, it is the wrong row, and where a part genuinely is type-specific the `notes` column
says so out loud.

This file is **research and vocabulary**. It is not a schema, and nothing here is implemented. It
exists so that whoever writes the `items[]` catalog is choosing from a real list instead of
remembering five fittings.

---

## THE SCALE RULE — read this before you use any number below

- **The player is 1.8 world units tall.** `scenes/shared/player.tscn` is a `CapsuleShape3D` of
  height 1.8 with the eye at 1.6. Structure Studio's mannequin matches and labels itself "1.8 m".
- Therefore **1 world unit = 1 metre for anything a human touches, stands on, ducks under, or
  grabs.** No exceptions in this file.
- **Hull geometry is drawn at 2× its catalog label.** `hull_28x10` displays as "14.0 × 5.0 m" and
  is drawn 28 units long. Beside the 1.8-unit figure it reads as a **28 m vessel**. The label is
  wrong about the world; the figure is right.
- **Every dimension in this file is in world units, sized against the figure.** A door is ~2.0
  tall. A bulwark is 1.0–1.2. A handrail is 1.1. A bollard is 0.6. A liferaft canister is 1.6 long.
  If a number here disagrees with a catalog display name, the catalog display name loses.

The failure mode this rule exists to prevent has already happened once:
`probe_trawler_bow_bulwark`'s wheelhouse was sized against the "14 m" label, came out at ~10% of
LOA where a real trawler is nearly double that, and **every number the gate records agreed with
every other number and they were all wrong together.** A human looking at a picture caught it.

## How to read the table

| Column | What it means |
|---|---|
| **object** | Correct nautical name. Common synonyms in parentheses — builders will search for those. |
| **category** | Which group the row belongs to (repeated per row so rows survive being copied out of context). |
| **why** | What it is FOR, concretely. Not "safety" — *"stops a person going over the side when the deck is wet."* A player placing this should understand why they are placing it. |
| **physical** | Form, material, typical colour, how it attaches, one piece or assembly. |
| **dimensions_units** | **World units, sized against the 1.8 figure.** A realistic RANGE where vessels differ, plus what the dimension scales with (beam? deck length? nothing?). Where a real-world figure is uncertain it says so rather than inventing precision. |
| **primitive** | Which of the five primitives from `REFERENCES.md` builds it — **spar**, **railing run**, **wire**, **sloped plate**, **sheer band** — or **box/room/deck** if today's primitives already cover it, or **new** with a note on what is missing. |
| **live_or_baked** | **baked** = merges into the static vessel skin. **live** = must stay an interactive node. Rule of thumb: anything the player operates, anything that emits light, anything that moves, anything gameplay counts by identity, and anything a rope or a physics body attaches to must stay live. Everything else bakes. |
| **compliance** | If it maps to something `resources/data/vessels/registrations/catalog.json` counts. The vocabulary that file and `scripts/ship/vessel_compliance.gd` actually understand today is: tags `mooring`, `nav_white`, `bulk_hold`, `crane`, `fishing`, `tow`, `cargo`; brick ids `light_nav_port`, `light_nav_stbd`, `light_nav_white`, `light_mast_white`; slots `helm`, `fishing`, `crane`, `mooring`; metrics `exposed_deck_cells`, `doors`, `cargo_cells`, `passenger_capacity`, `equipment_rating`; capability `has_cabin`. Most rows are honestly "—". |
| **notes** | Counts per vessel, usual placement, what it must not collide with, how it varies by vessel type. |

**A note on the primitive column.** Where a row says "new", that is a finding, not a complaint —
the last section of this file collects them. A row that can be assembled from two or three existing
primitives is marked with the assembly, e.g. `spar ×2 + wire` for an A-frame, because that is
exactly the kit-versus-model-set distinction: the catalog entry saves the player the assembly, it
does not do something the player could not do by hand.

## 1. Hull structure — shell, framing, and the lines below the deck edge

Most of this is invisible on a finished vessel, but the *lines* it produces (chine, knuckle,
sheer, rubbing strake) are the entire reason a real hull reads as fast, heavy, or workmanlike and
our extruded box does not.

| object | category | why | physical | dimensions_units | primitive | live_or_baked | compliance | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shell plating** (hull plate, strake) | Structure | The watertight skin. Everything else on the hull is attached to it or cut out of it. | Flat or single-curvature steel/aluminium plate, welded edge to edge into a continuous surface; runs fore-and-aft in bands called strakes. Painted; colour changes at the boot-top and at any paint region boundary. | Plate thickness 0.008–0.020 (8–20 mm) on a working hull of 20–60 units LOA; strake width 1.2–2.0. Thickness scales with vessel length and is essentially invisible except at a cut edge. | box/room/deck (the hull loft already emits this) | baked | — | Thickness only matters where you see a cut edge — a freeing port, a hawse, a boarding gate. Draw those edges with the real thickness or the hull reads as paper. |
| **Frame** (rib, transverse frame) | Structure | Holds the shell plating to its shape and carries the sea's pressure into the ship's backbone. Sets the whole rhythm of an interior. | Bent flat bar or angle/bulb-flat, welded inboard of the shell, running from keel up to deck. Bare or painted the interior colour. One per station. | Frame spacing 0.5–0.9 (call it 0.6 for a workboat); frame depth 0.10–0.25 into the space. Spacing scales with vessel size but only weakly — it converges on 0.6. | sloped plate (a thin quad following the shell) or **new**: a swept profile along a curve | baked | — | Only visible in unlined spaces — engine room, chain locker, fish hold, forepeak. In an accommodation space they are hidden behind lining, so do not draw them there. |
| **Web frame** | Structure | Every fourth or fifth frame is a deep one, because a plain frame is not strong enough on its own over a long span. Gives an engine room its characteristic ribbed look. | Deep plate frame with a face flat on its free edge and lightening holes cut through the web; welded to shell, deck and floor. | Web depth 0.4–0.8; spaced 3–5 ordinary frame spacings apart, so 2.4–4.5 between webs. Depth scales with beam. | sloped plate + circular cutouts (**new**: plate with holes) | baked | — | The lightening holes are the recognisable part. A web frame drawn as a solid plate looks like a bulkhead. |
| **Longitudinal stringer** (side girder) | Structure | Stops the shell panels between frames from oil-canning; ties the frames together fore-and-aft. | Flat bar or bulb flat welded to the inside of the shell, running the length of the vessel, notched through each frame. | Depth 0.10–0.20, spaced 0.8–1.5 apart vertically up the side. Scales with hull depth. | sloped plate strip | baked | — | Interior-only. Their intersections with frames make the egg-crate pattern of an unlined hold. |
| **Floor** (bottom transverse) | Structure | The transverse plate across the bottom of the hull at each frame, tying port to starboard through the keel. | Plate with limber holes at the bottom corners so bilge water can drain aft to a single suction point. | Depth 0.3–0.8 above the shell; at each frame or every second frame. | sloped plate with cutouts (**new**) | baked | — | Limber holes matter for anyone modelling a visible bilge — without them the bilge is a series of unconnected puddles. |
| **Keel** (bar keel / flat plate keel) | Structure | The backbone. Also the part that takes the ground when the vessel dries out or sits on a slipway. | Either a solid bar standing proud below the shell, or a thicker centreline plate flush with it. Antifouled below the waterline. | Bar keel 0.15–0.35 deep, 0.10–0.20 wide, full length. Flat keel strake width 1.0–1.6. Scales with LOA. | box/room/deck | baked | — | Only ever seen out of the water — slipway, drydock, careened. Worth having for a shipyard scene. |
| **Stem** (stem bar, stemhead) | Structure | The leading edge of the vessel. Its rake is most of what makes a hull read as fast, workmanlike, or old. | Rounded bar or a plated knuckle where port and starboard shell plates meet. Often carries a half-round guard, sometimes a stemhead roller. | Rake anywhere from vertical (0°, plumb-bow workboat) through 20–35° (typical) to 45°+ (fast ferry). Height = full hull depth at bow, 2.5–6.0. | sloped plate | baked | — | R1's very fine sharply raked bow and R2's flared workboat stem are the same primitive at different angles. Do not hard-code 45°. |
| **Forefoot knuckle** | Structure | Where the fine bow section turns into the fuller forward hull. It is a *line*, and it catches light differently from either surface it separates. | A hard crease in the shell plating, running from near the stem down and aft. No added material — it is a change of plate angle. | Runs 3–10 along the forward hull, at 25–60% of hull depth. Scales with LOA. | sloped plate pair meeting at an edge | baked | — | R1's is pronounced. This is one of the four things `REFERENCES.md` names as making the real thing read fast where our box does not. |
| **Hard chine** | Structure | The crease between bottom and topsides on a planing or semi-planing hull. Sheds spray and gives lift. | A continuous hard edge, sometimes with a spray rail on it. The two facets it separates take light completely differently — this is why a chined hull looks faceted. | Runs full length; sits 0.4–1.5 above the waterline amidships, rising toward the bow. Scales with hull depth. | sloped plate pair | baked | — | Round-bilge hulls have none — this is a hull-family choice, not an add-on. Offer both. |
| **Knuckle line** | Structure | A deliberate crease in the topsides above the waterline, usually to increase deck width without increasing waterline beam. | Same as a chine but higher up and often only over part of the length. Frequently a paint boundary sits on it. | 0.3–1.0 below the deck edge; may run only the forward third. | sloped plate pair | baked | — | Paint region boundaries love to land on a knuckle, because the crease hides the line's imperfection. |
| **Sheer strake** | Structure | The topmost strake of shell plating, where the side meets the deck. It is thicker than the rest because it is the most highly stressed plate on the ship. | A visibly heavier band of plate at the deck edge, often a different colour from the topsides, often the top edge of the hull's main paint region. | Width 0.8–1.6; thickness 1.3–1.6× ordinary shell. Width scales with hull depth. | sheer band | baked | — | Not the same thing as a rubbing strake — the sheer strake is structure, the rubbing strake is sacrificial. Both can be present. |
| **Rubbing strake** (belting, rubbing band, fender bar) | Structure | Takes the hit when the vessel comes alongside a quay, so the shell plating does not. Sacrificial and replaceable. | A half-round bar, D-section rubber, or a fabricated steel box section, bolted or welded proud of the topsides. Often black or bright contrast colour. Continuous, following the sheer. | Section 0.10–0.30 tall, standing 0.08–0.25 proud. One or two bands. Height on the hull: at or just below the deck edge, 0.3–1.0 down. | sheer band (a polyline swept section following the deck-edge curve) | baked | — | R2 has a visible one. On workboats it is a signature element — a trawler without belting looks like a yacht. Must not be interrupted by the boarding gate; it usually steps around it. |
| **Bilge keel** | Structure | Damps rolling. A vessel without them rolls harder and faster in a beam sea. | A long thin plate fin welded along the turn of the bilge at about 45°, tapered at both ends. Antifouled. | Length 25–40% of LOA; projects 0.2–0.6. Scales with beam. | sloped plate | baked | — | Underwater — only seen on a slipway or in clear water. Cheap to include and instantly reads "real ship". |
| **Bulbous bow** | Structure | Cancels part of the bow wave and reduces resistance at the design speed. Only pays off on vessels that run at a constant speed. | A rounded protrusion forward at or below the waterline, faired into the stem. Antifouled, with a draft-mark ladder often just aft. | Length 2–6 forward of the stem's waterline point; diameter 1.5–4.0. Scales with LOA and is wrong on small craft. | **new**: a rounded blister — no existing primitive makes a compound-curved bulb | baked | — | Do not offer this on hulls under ~30 units. A bulb on a 15 m fishing boat is a modelling error, not a style choice. |
| **Skeg** | Structure | Protects the propeller and rudder from grounding damage and gives the vessel directional stability. | A vertical fin below the hull aft, sometimes carrying the bottom rudder bearing. | Depth 0.4–1.5 below the hull line, length 2–8. Scales with LOA. | sloped plate | baked | — | Trawlers and workboats have prominent ones; fast catamarans generally do not. |
| **Spray rail** (spray chine, spray strake) | Structure | Knocks spray down instead of letting it climb the topsides and reach the deck and the windows. | A small triangular or L-section strip welded to the forward topsides, angled slightly down. | Section 0.05–0.15; runs the forward 30–50% of LOA. Nearly constant across vessel sizes. | sloped plate strip | baked | — | Usually one per side, sometimes two stacked. Fast craft have them; slow displacement boats often do not. |
| **Demihull** | Structure | On a catamaran, one of the two hulls. Each is narrow enough to have very low wave-making resistance. | A complete slender hull; the pair are joined by the bridging structure. Often the paint region wraps around each independently. | On a 45-unit cat: each demihull 3.5–5.0 beam, hulls set 10–13 apart centre to centre. Scales with overall beam. | box/room/deck (hull loft) | baked | — | `hull_45x16_cat` already gives this. R1 and R3 are both this family. |
| **Bridging deck / wet deck** | Structure | The structure spanning between the demihulls, carrying the deck and superstructure. Its underside must clear the wave crests. | A flat or arched panel between the hulls; the arch (tunnel) is the loudest feature seen from ahead. | Clearance above waterline 1.2–3.0 depending on service; arch rise 0.5–1.5. Scales with beam. | sloped plate / **new**: an arched panel | baked | — | R1 has "a raised tunnel arch". Get the clearance wrong and the vessel looks like a raft or looks like it is on stilts. |
| **Watertight bulkhead** | Structure | Divides the hull into compartments so one hole does not sink the whole vessel. Legally required at specific stations. | Plain stiffened steel plate, welded all round, painted. Penetrated only by watertight doors and sealed pipe/cable glands. | Full hull depth, so 2.5–6.0 tall × full beam. Thickness 0.008–0.014. Position is set by rule, not by taste. | box/room/deck (a wall) | baked (the bulkhead) / **live** (its door) | — | The collision bulkhead is the important one — 5–8% of LOA aft of the stem. Everything forward of it is the forepeak, which is why forepeaks are always small and always awkward. |
| **Joiner bulkhead** (partition, non-structural bulkhead) | Structure | Divides accommodation into cabins. Carries no load and can be put anywhere. | Light panel — steel sheet, plywood, or a proprietary sandwich panel — in a frame, faced with lining. Light colours. | 2.0–2.4 tall (deckhead height), thickness 0.05–0.10. Height scales with the deck-to-deck spacing, nothing else. | box/room/deck | baked | — | This is the one a player will place most often and it is already fully supported. Keep the thickness distinction from watertight bulkheads — it is a 4× difference and it reads. |
| **Deep tank boundary** | Structure | Forms a fuel, water or ballast tank inside the hull. Same plate as a bulkhead but stiffened much more heavily. | Heavily stiffened plate with a manhole in it and a sounding pipe running up from it. Tank top often forms the sole of the space above. | Tank height 1.0–2.5; manhole 0.4 × 0.6 oval. | box/room/deck | baked (boundary) / **live** (manhole cover, sounding cap) | — | The manhole is the visible evidence. A tank top with no manhole is a floor, not a tank. |
| **Doubler plate** | Structure | A patch of extra plate welded on where a fitting concentrates load into the shell or deck — under a bollard, a crane pedestal, a davit, a padeye. | A rectangle, disc, or diamond of plate with rounded corners, welded flat to the surface with a visible fillet weld all round. Painted with the surface it sits on. | 0.4–1.5 across, 0.008–0.020 thick. Should extend 1.5–3× the footprint of whatever sits on it. | sloped plate (flat quad, small offset) | baked | — | Excellent detail-per-unit-cost: a bollard on a bare deck looks pasted on, a bollard on a doubler looks engineered. Rounded corners matter — square corners crack in reality and look wrong. |
| **Insert plate** | Structure | Where an opening is cut in the shell, a thicker plate is let *into* the surrounding plating rather than doubled on top. | A flush rectangle of heavier plate, visible only as a weld seam outline around an opening. | Follows the opening + 0.3–0.6 margin. | sloped plate / weld seam line | baked | — | Flush, unlike a doubler. Good around hawse pipes, sea chests, and stern-ramp hinges. |
| **Bracket / gusset** (knee) | Structure | Carries load around a corner — where a frame meets a deck beam, where a stanchion meets the deck, where a bulwark stay meets the sheer strake. | A triangular plate with a curved (snipe) free edge, welded into the corner. Sometimes with a face flat on the curve. | 0.2–0.8 on each leg. Scales with the member it braces. | sloped plate (triangle) | baked | — | The single highest-value small detail in the whole file. Real steelwork is full of these; CG steelwork never has them, and that is most of why CG steelwork looks like cardboard. |
| **Pillar** (stanchion, structural post) | Structure | Carries the deck above down to the deck below where there is no bulkhead to do it. | A round or square section post, deck to deckhead, with a bracketed head and foot. Painted the interior colour. | Diameter 0.10–0.25, height = deck clearance 2.0–2.6. Spacing 2.5–4.0. Diameter scales with load, height with deck spacing. | spar | baked | — | Distinct from a railing stanchion — this one holds the ship up. In a fish hold or a car deck they are a defining visual rhythm and a genuine obstruction the player must walk around. |
| **Sea chest** | Structure | The recessed opening in the shell that feeds seawater to engine cooling, fire pumps, and ballast. | A recess in the shell closed by a coarse grating, with a grid of bars. Below the waterline, so antifouled, with an anode nearby. | Grating 0.5–1.2 square; usually two (high and low suction). | sloped plate + grating (**new**: grating/grille) | baked | — | Slipway detail. The anode beside it is nearly universal because dissimilar metals meet there. |
| **Bow thruster tunnel** | Structure | A transverse tunnel with a propeller in it, so the vessel can move its bow sideways without a tug. | A circular opening in each side of the bow with a grid or bars across it, faired into the shell with a rounded lip. | Diameter 0.6–1.6, centreline 1.0–2.5 below the waterline. Diameter scales with vessel displacement. | **new**: a circular hull penetration | baked (tunnel) / **live** if the thruster is operable | — | Nearly universal on modern workboats and ferries; wrong on a period trawler. Always paired port and starboard by being one tunnel. |
| **Anchor pocket** (anchor recess, hawse recess) | Structure | Lets the anchor stow flush against the hull instead of hanging out where it will damage the quay or the hull. | A shaped recess in the bow plating with the hawse pipe opening at its top and a wear-plate face where the fluke lands. Nearly always with heavy weld seams and paint wear around it. | Recess 1.0–2.5 across, 0.15–0.5 deep. Scales with anchor size, which scales with vessel size. | sloped plate recess | baked | — | R1 has one. On small craft the anchor sits on a bow roller instead — offer both, they are alternatives, not companions. |

## 2. Deck, deck edge, and the enclosure above it

The deck edge is where the vessel's identity lives. Bulwark versus guardrail is the single biggest
"what kind of boat is this" decision a builder makes, and the cap rail carries the sheer that
`REFERENCES.md` proved the hull loft cannot.

| object | category | why | physical | dimensions_units | primitive | live_or_baked | compliance | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Deck plating** (deck, weather deck) | Structure | The surface people and cargo stand on, and the top of the watertight envelope. | Stiffened steel/aluminium plate, cambered slightly so water runs to the sides. Coated with non-slip; painted in work areas, sometimes bare in cargo areas. | Thickness 0.008–0.015; camber 0.5–2% of beam, so 0.05–0.20 rise at the centreline on a 10-unit beam. Deck-to-deck height 2.2–3.0. | box/room/deck | baked | `exposed_deck_cells` (weather deck area, drives the `fishing_vessel` catch-deck rule) | Camber is subtle but its absence is why flat CG decks look like floors. If you cannot do camber, at least slope toward the scuppers. |
| **Deck beam** | Structure | Carries the deck plating across the ship between frames. | Bulb flat or T-section under the deck, at each frame, bracketed to the frames at each end. | Depth 0.15–0.35, at frame spacing 0.5–0.9. | sloped plate strip | baked | — | Only seen from below, in unlined spaces — which is exactly where a player will be standing in an engine room. |
| **Camber / deck crown** | Structure | Sheds water off the deck to the sides, where the scuppers and freeing ports are. | A gentle transverse arch in the deck plating. Not perceptible standing on it; very perceptible looking along it. | Rise 1/50 of beam is the classic rule: 0.10–0.30 on typical beams. | **new**: a curved deck surface | baked | — | If the deck is dead flat, water pools and the vessel looks like an architectural model. |
| **Sheer** (sheerline) | Structure | The fore-and-aft curve of the deck edge. Keeps the bow dry and is the vessel's single most identifying line. | Not an object — the curve of the deck edge in profile. Usually highest at the bow, lowest around 60–70% aft, rising again at the stern. | Bow rise 0.8–2.5 above the amidships deck line on a workboat; near zero on a fast ferry with a straight sheer. Scales with LOA and with how rough the intended water is. | **sheer band** (deck-edge polyline with per-station height) | baked | — | R2's defining line. `REFERENCES.md` records that this **cannot come from the hull loft** and needs the sheer band primitive. A reverse sheer (rising aft) exists too and reads as modern/military. |
| **Bulwark** | Structure | A solid wall around the deck edge. Stops people and gear going over the side, and stops green water sweeping the deck. The workboat answer to the deck edge. | Continuous plate wall standing on the deck edge, stiffened on the inboard face by stays, capped by a rail bar. Usually painted the hull or superstructure colour, or a contrast band. | Height **1.0–1.2** (regulatory minimum is 1.0 m and real vessels sit just above it); occasionally 1.4+ on a fishing vessel's working deck. Thickness 0.008–0.014. Height is essentially constant across vessel sizes — **it scales with the human, not the ship.** | sheer band (carries the sheer) + a plate wall | baked | — | This is the number people get wrong most often. A bulwark drawn at 0.6 makes a 40 m ship look like a bathtub toy; at 2.0 it makes the crew look like children. Freeing ports must be cut through it. |
| **Bulwark stay** (bulwark bracket) | Structure | Braces the bulwark plate against a boarding sea. Without them a bulwark folds. | A triangular plate knee, welded between the inboard face of the bulwark and the deck, snipe-cut on the free edge. | 0.5–0.8 tall × 0.3–0.6 on the deck; spaced 1.2–2.0 apart. Spacing is roughly every second frame. | sloped plate (triangle), repeated along a run | baked | — | Trips people. Real crews know where they are; a player walking a working deck should have to notice them. Never place a stay where a freeing port is. |
| **Cap rail** (gunwale bar, bulwark rail, capping) | Structure | Finishes the raw top edge of the bulwark plate so it is not a knife edge, and stiffens it along its length. This is the band that carries the sheer curve. | A flat bar, half-round, or small pipe laid along the top of the bulwark and welded. Very frequently a *contrast colour* — cream, yellow, varnished timber, black. | Section 0.06–0.15 wide × 0.03–0.08 thick; top edge at 1.0–1.2. Constant with vessel size. | **sheer band** | baked | — | R2's "cream/yellow bulwark cap band" is exactly this. It is the cheapest way to make a hull look finished, and one of the strongest identity elements available. |
| **Guardrail / stanchion run** | Safety | The open-tube alternative to a bulwark. Stops a person going over the side when the deck is wet, while letting water off the deck and weight out of the vessel. | Vertical posts (stanchions) socketed or welded to the deck edge, carrying 2–3 horizontal tube courses, sometimes with a wire bottom course. Galvanised or painted white. | Top rail height **1.0–1.1**; courses at roughly 0.35 / 0.70 / 1.05. Stanchion pitch 1.2–1.8, tube diameter 0.03–0.05. **Constant with vessel size** — it is human-scaled. | **railing run** | baked (usually) — **live** where a gate opens | — | R1's "tube guardrails everywhere" are called out as the single most identifying small detail. Gaps between courses must be small enough that a person cannot fall through — that is why there are three, not one. |
| **Stanchion** (railing post) | Safety | The post that holds the rails up. Sometimes removable so cargo can be worked over the side. | Pipe or bar, 3 holes drilled for the rail courses, either welded to a base plate or dropped into a deck socket with a retaining pin. | Height 1.05–1.15 above deck, diameter 0.03–0.05, base plate 0.10–0.15 square. | spar (short) | baked, or **live** if removable | — | Distinct from a structural pillar — this one holds a person in, not the ship up. A socket-mounted stanchion needs the socket modelled, or it looks glued down. |
| **Cap rail / bulwark boarding gate** | Circulation | A removable or hinged section of the deck edge so people can board from a quay or a pontoon. | A hinged panel in the bulwark, or a removable pair of stanchions with a chain across, or a proper gate with a latch. Often with a contrasting-colour surround and a grab rail each side. | Opening 0.8–1.2 wide, full bulwark height. Width is fixed by human passage, not vessel size. | railing run (with a gap) + **live** hinged panel | **live** | — | R1 has one. Every vessel that carries people needs at least one per side. It must not land on a bulwark stay, a freeing port, or a mooring fairlead. |
| **Freeing port** (scupper port, wash port) | Structure | Lets water that has come aboard drain off the deck fast. A deck full of water is a stability problem, not a nuisance. | A rectangular hole cut through the bottom of the bulwark, with a rounded-corner insert plate around it, often with a hinged flap or bars so cargo and fish do not go out with the water. | 0.4–1.2 wide × 0.2–0.35 tall, sill on the deck. Total area is set by rule from bulwark length; count 2–6 per side. | sloped plate cutout | baked (port) / **live** (flap, if animated) | — | Required by law wherever there is a bulwark. Rounded corners — a square-cornered hole in a plate is a crack waiting to happen and every real one is radiused. |
| **Coaming** (hatch coaming, door coaming, sill) | Structure | A raised lip around any opening in a weather deck, so water running along the deck does not run into the hole. | A short plate wall standing around the opening, capped and stiffened, with the hatch or cover landing on it. | Weather-deck hatch coaming **0.45–0.60** tall (rule minimum ~0.45 on an exposed deck); door sills 0.15–0.30; internal coamings 0.05–0.15.
