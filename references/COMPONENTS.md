# COMPONENTS.md — the working-vessel parts catalogue

A reference dataset of **every object that plausibly exists on a working vessel**, written as a
**kit**, not a model set.

Owner direction, 2026-08-09: *"do not tailor these boats, make parts that people can build custom
ships from."* So every row here is the **general form** of a thing — "cleat, 0.15–0.45 m, scales
with line size" — never "the cleat off that Norwegian trawler". If a row only makes sense on one
vessel type, it is the wrong row, and where a part genuinely is type-specific the `notes` column
says so out loud.

This file is **research and vocabulary**. It is not a schema, and nothing here is implemented. It
exists so that whoever writes the `items[]` catalog is choosing from a real list instead of
remembering five fittings.

---

## THE SCALE RULE — read this before you use any number below

**One world unit is one metre. One deck-grid cell is 0.5 m — two cells per metre.**

- **The player is 1.8 m tall** (`scenes/shared/player.tscn`, capsule 1.8, eye 1.6) — that is
  **3.6 cells**. Everything a human touches is sized against that figure. Every metre figure in
  the tables below is a real metre; the cell figure beside it is that number doubled.
- The cell is a **build resolution, not a size**. A 1 m grid was too coarse to build detail on,
  so it was halved. Nothing about a vessel's physical dimensions changed when it was.
- **Hull `loa_m` / `beam_m` / `depth_m` are real metres.** `hull_28x10` is a **28 × 10 m**
  vessel — 56 × 20 cells. `hull_45x16_cat` is **45 × 16 m**.

> **Corrected 2026-08-09, after most of this file was written.** An earlier instruction to this
> dataset said a 28 × 10 grid meant a 14 × 5 m vessel. That was wrong, and it came from two wrong
> artefacts in the hull catalog — a display string reading "14.0 × 5.0 m" and a note claiming
> "in-world metres (2× real)", both leftovers from a scaling decision that never landed.
> Five numeric fields disagreed with them: `depth_m` 5.6 (a 14 m boat is ~2.8), `displacement_t`
> 256 (a 14 m boat computes to 41–50), `default_shaft_power_kw` 1871 (a 14 m boat is ~400) — and
> `tests/vessel_registration_test.gd` had been calling it the "28×10 m starter" all along. The
> labels were corrected to match the data; no physics was touched.
>
> **The per-row numbers below are unaffected** — they were always sized against the 1.8 m figure,
> which is the correct basis, and the cell column is simply metres × 2.
>
> **What this does change is hull matching.** Every hull in the catalog is 28 m or longer, so
> none of the three reference vessels has a close match: R1 (~23 m) and R3 (~22 m) sit between
> hulls, and R2 (a small inshore cutter, ~15 m) has nothing near it at all. **The catalog has no
> small hulls.** That is a real gap, not a labelling artefact, and it matters more than it looks:
> a player wanting to build anything like R2 currently cannot start from a hull that fits.

### How dimensions are written

Every dimension is given twice:

> **1.0–1.2 m** *(2.0–2.4 cells)*

The metre figure is the real-world size against a 1.8 m person. The cell figure is the same number
at 2 cells/m, so a builder can see immediately whether a part fits the grid.

### Sub-cell parts — the load-bearing distinction

Anything smaller than **0.5 m in its governing dimension cannot be placed on the grid at all.**
Cleats, fairleads, chocks, light fixtures, blocks, sheaves, handrail stanchions, portlights, most
hardware — these are **sub-cell** and need **free positioning with float coordinates**, not cell
snapping. Every such row is flagged **`SUB-CELL`** at the head of its `notes`.

This is not a detail. It decides which half of the parts catalog needs an off-grid placement model
and which half can snap, and it is a large fraction of the catalog — roughly every fitting a human
operates with their hands.

### The failure mode this section exists to prevent

`probe_trawler_bow_bulwark`'s wheelhouse was sized against the wrong reading of the hull scale,
came out at ~10% of LOA where a real trawler is nearly double that, and **every number the gate
records agreed with every other number and they were all wrong together.** A human looking at a
picture caught it. Numbers that agree with each other are not evidence.

## How to read the table

| Column | What it means |
|---|---|
| **object** | Correct nautical name. Common synonyms in parentheses — builders will search for those. |
| **category** | Which group the row belongs to (repeated per row so rows survive being copied out of context). |
| **why** | What it is FOR, concretely. Not "safety" — *"stops a person going over the side when the deck is wet."* A player placing this should understand why they are placing it. |
| **physical** | Form, material, typical colour, how it attaches, one piece or assembly. |
| **dimensions_units** | **Real metres against the 1.8 m figure, then the same in grid cells at 2 cells/m.** A realistic RANGE where vessels differ, plus what the dimension scales with (beam? deck length? nothing?). Where a real-world figure is uncertain it says so rather than inventing precision. |
| **primitive** | Which of the five primitives from `REFERENCES.md` builds it — **spar**, **railing run**, **wire**, **sloped plate**, **sheer band** — or **box/room/deck** if today's primitives already cover it, or **new** with a note on what is missing. |
| **live_or_baked** | **baked** = merges into the static vessel skin. **live** = must stay an interactive node. Rule of thumb: anything the player operates, anything that emits light, anything that moves, anything gameplay counts by identity, and anything a rope or physics body attaches to must stay live. Everything else bakes. |
| **compliance** | If it maps to something `resources/data/vessels/registrations/catalog.json` counts. The vocabulary that file and `scripts/ship/vessel_compliance.gd` actually understand today is: tags `mooring`, `nav_white`, `bulk_hold`, `crane`, `fishing`, `tow`, `cargo`; brick ids `light_nav_port`, `light_nav_stbd`, `light_nav_white`, `light_mast_white`; slots `helm`, `fishing`, `crane`, `mooring`; metrics `exposed_deck_cells`, `doors`, `cargo_cells`, `passenger_capacity`, `equipment_rating`; capability `has_cabin`. Most rows are honestly "—". |
| **notes** | **`SUB-CELL`** flag where applicable, then counts per vessel, usual placement, what it must not collide with, how it varies by vessel type. |

**A note on the primitive column.** Where a row says "new", that is a finding, not a complaint —
the last section of this file collects them. A row that can be assembled from two or three existing
primitives is marked with the assembly, e.g. `spar ×2 + wire` for an A-frame, because that is
exactly the kit-versus-model-set distinction: the catalog entry saves the player the assembly, it
does not do something the player could not do by hand.

**Vessel size assumed throughout.** Unless a row says otherwise, "a working vessel" here means
**8–30 m LOA** — the range the hull catalog covers under the 0.5 m cell convention. Rows that only
make sense above or below that range say so.

## 1. Hull structure — shell, framing, and the lines below the deck edge

Most of this is invisible on a finished vessel, but the *lines* it produces (chine, knuckle,
sheer, rubbing strake) are the entire reason a real hull reads as fast, heavy, or workmanlike and
our extruded box does not.

## 2. Deck, deck edge, and the enclosure above it

The deck edge is where the vessel's identity lives. Bulwark versus guardrail is the biggest
"what kind of boat is this" decision a builder makes, and the cap rail carries the sheer that
`REFERENCES.md` proved the hull loft cannot.

| object | category | why | physical | dimensions_units | primitive | live_or_baked | compliance | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Deck plating** (deck, weather deck) | Structure | The surface people and cargo stand on, and the top of the watertight envelope. | Stiffened plate, cambered so water runs to the sides. | **Deck beam** | Structure | Carries the deck plating across the ship between frames. | Bulb flat or T-section under the deck at each frame, bracketed to the frames at each end. | Depth **80–200 mm** *(0.16–0.4 cells)*, at frame spacing **0.5–0.6 m** *(1–1.2 cells)*. | sloped plate strip | baked | — | **SUB-CELL** in depth. Almost none of this is geometry. It is nearly all **surface**, which makes it the cheapest
identity in the file once colour lives in vertex data — `REFERENCES.md` records that two of the
three reference vessels get most of their identity from paint, not from shape.

## 5. Ground tackle, mooring and fendering

This is the densest compliance category in the file. `general_vessel` requires **at least four
`mooring` tagged points**, inherited by every other registration — so bollards, bitts, cleats and
staghorns are the one fitting family the rules system already counts today.

Note how much of it is **sub-cell**: a cleat is 0.3 m long. Mooring hardware is the clearest case
for a free-positioned item model, because it is hardware sized for hands and rope, not for a grid.

## 6. Deck machinery and hydraulics

Powered gear. Almost all of it is **live** — it turns, it hauls, it has a control lever a player
can put a hand on. It is also the visual anchor of a working deck: a deck with a winch on it reads
as a workplace, and the same deck without one reads as a patio.

## 7. Masts, rigging and lifting gear

`REFERENCES.md` ranks "mast + radar" and "crane / derrick / gallows" at the top of the list of
things whose absence explains "it looks wrong", and says outright that **a trawler without rigging
is a hull with a shed on it**. This section is where the **spar** and **wire** primitives earn
their place, and where almost everything is an *assembly* rather than a single part.

## 8. Cargo, holds and fishing gear

The two things a working vessel exists to carry: cargo and catch. This is the section with the
most direct compliance hooks — `bulk_hold`, `cargo_cells`, `exposed_deck_cells` and the `fishing`

carries a "which vessels" note.

## 9. Navigation, signalling and communications

The compliance-heaviest section after mooring. `general_vessel` — inherited by *every* other
registration — requires a port light on the port side, a starboard light on the starboard side, one
or two `nav_white` lights, and the white light **above** the sidelights (`white_above_sidelights`).
That last rule is a *height* comparison, which means nav lights cannot be baked: their world
positions have to survive into the compliance check.

Lights are also almost all **sub-cell** — a sidelight is a 0.25 m box — while the rule that governs
them is about where they sit on a vessel tens of metres long. That gap is the argument for
free-positioned items in a single sentence.

| object | category | why | physical | dimensions_units | primitive | live_or_baked | compliance | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Vessels over 50 m carry two, a lower forward and a higher aft — the catalog already allows `nav_white` max 2. |

## 10. Safety and lifesaving

Almost none of this maps to the registration catalog today — there is no `liferaft` tag, no
`lifebuoy` count, no muster rule. That is a real gap: the rules system knows about navigation
lights and mooring points and nothing at all about whether anyone survives. Flagged per row where
the omission is conspicuous.

Visually this is a high-value section because lifesaving gear is **the only brightly coloured
equipment on most vessels** — white canisters, orange rings, red bottles — against grey and hull
colour.

## 11. Machinery, propulsion, exhaust and ventilation

The engine room is a space a player can stand in, and it is the one interior that is *not* lined —
frames, pipes, cable trays and gratings are all exposed. Above deck, the funnel and the vents are
a large part of a vessel's silhouette: `REFERENCES.md` ranks exhaust stack fourth on the list of

## 13. Interior — accommodation, helm and fit-out

Every dimension here is set by the human body, and the body is 1.8 m. Seat height 0.45 m, worktop
0.9 m, bunk 1.95 m long, deckhead 2.0 m. **None of it scales with the vessel**, which means an
interior is the one place where getting the scale wrong is immediately obvious — and the one place
the "hull is 2× its label" confusion did the most damage.

This is also where `passenger_capacity`, `helm` and `has_cabin` live, so it is directly load-bearing
for the `passenger_vessel` registration.

| object | category | why | physical | dimensions_units | primitive | live_or_baked | compliance | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Helm console** (dashboard, steering console) | Interior | Where the vessel is driven from. ## What this implies for the primitives

**344 parts across 13 categories.** Of those:

- **164 are sub-cell** — smaller than 0.5 m in their governing dimension.
- **222 must stay live** — they move, emit light, are operated, or carry a counted attribute.
- **98 touch compliance** in some way; the rest are honestly "—".
- **59 name something the five primitives cannot express.**

The five primitives — spar, railing run, wire, sloped plate, sheer band — were derived from three
photographs, and they hold up better than they had any right to. Between them they build masts,
derricks, gallows, A-frames, davits, cranes, railings, rigging, lifelines, hoses, bulwarks, cap
rails, rubbing strakes, raked windscreens, chines, knuckles and transoms. That is most of the
visible difference between a box and a vessel, and it was five ideas.

But this dataset is four times larger than the evidence they came from, and it breaks them in
specific, fixable ways. Here is what it found, worst first.

---

### 1. The grid is the wrong home for half the catalog — and that is the biggest finding

**164 of 344 parts are smaller than one cell.** Not marginally: a cleat is 0.3 m long and 0.1 m
tall, which is 0.6 × 0.2 cells. A window mullion is 0.08 m wide — one sixth of a cell. A sidelight
is 0.2 m. A guardrail tube is 30 mm. A pipe is 25 mm.

These are not decorations. They include **every mooring fitting the compliance system counts**
(bollard, bitt, cleat, fairlead), **every navigation light the rules check by side and by height**,
and the mullions that decide whether a wheelhouse has wraparound glass or arrow slits.

So the parts catalog splits cleanly in two, and the split is not stylistic:

| | snaps to cells | needs float placement |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | spaces, decks, bulkheads, holds, deckhouses, hatches, tanks, stairs, cargo footprints | fittings, hardware, lights, rails, pipes, instruments — anything sized for a hand or an eye |
| **Roughly** | ~180 rows | ~164 rows |
| **Why** | they are *volumes*, and 0.5 m granularity is right for a volume | they are *objects on a surface*, and their position is set by the structure they attach to, not by a lattice |

A single placement model cannot serve both. **`items[]` needs float position, float rotation, and a
surface-attachment reference** (which deck edge, which bulwark run, which mast, at what distance
along it) — not integer cells. The `probe_ferry_catamaran` fixture already records that `items[]`
has "no reader, no catalog, and integer-cell positions"; this dataset says the integer cells are
not a limitation to work around, they are the wrong model for half the content.

**Two grid facts worth keeping.** Frame spacing (0.5–0.6 m) lands on almost exactly one cell, and a
20 ft ISO container (6.058 × 2.438 × 2.591 m) is 12.1 × 4.9 × 5.2 cells. Both near-fits are worth
exploiting rather than fighting.

---

### 2. Sheer band is secretly the most valuable primitive, and it is underspecified

As written it is "deck-edge polyline with per-station height" — a sheer carrier. But look at what
in this dataset is *the same operation*: a small constant cross-section swept along a long 3D path.

Rubbing strake. Cap rail. Sheer strake. Boot top. Permanent D-fender. Spray rail. Longitudinal
stringer. Chainplate straps. Deck edge striping. Hose and cable runs. Pipe runs. Weld seams.

That is **at least a dozen rows in this file**, several of them independently identified as
high-value ("the cheapest way to make a hull look finished", "the difference between a working boat
and a yacht"). Generalising sheer band from *a deck-edge height curve* to **a swept profile along
an arbitrary 3D polyline, with a selectable section (flat bar, half-round, D, L, tube, box)**
absorbs all of them into one primitive with two parameters.

**This is the single highest-leverage change available.** It costs one generalisation and it pays
for a dozen parts, most of them sub-cell and therefore otherwise homeless.

---

### 3. Spar needs a polyline, not two endpoints

Two endpoints handles masts, stanchions, posts, booms, bollards, davit arms — a lot. It cannot
handle:

- **Pipe runs** with three bends (§12, and there are dozens per vessel)
- **Goose-neck vent heads** — a pipe bent 180° at the top
- **Cowl vents** — a curved trumpet
- **Curved davit arms** — the classic davit is a single curved tube
- **Ladder stringers** that step around an obstruction
- **Handrails** that follow a deckhouse round a corner

Every one of these becomes 3–10 separate items under a two-endpoint spar, which multiplies both
authoring effort and item count for no gain. **A spar defined on a polyline with a radius and an
optional per-node taper** solves all six, and degenerates to today's spar when the polyline has two
nodes.

---

### 4. Wire's sag parameter must reach zero, and chain is not wire

Wire is well-chosen and this dataset uses it for more than rigging: mooring lines, hydraulic hose
bundles, shore power cables, flag halyards, guardrail gate chains, jackstays, lashings, VHF handset
leads. The **sag parameter carries most of the storytelling** — a taut topping lift and a slack
coiled runner are the same object at two settings, and the difference reads as "working" versus
"stowed".

Two problems:

- **Standing rigging must be dead straight.** A shroud drawn with mooring-line sag reads as broken
  rigging, which is worse than no rigging. Sag must be able to be exactly zero.
- **Chain is not a smooth curve.** Anchor cable, lashing chain and gripes read as chain *because of
  the links*. A smooth wire on a catenary gets the shape right and the material wrong. This needs
  either a segmented variant or an accepted shading treatment — flagged as unsolved, not solved.

Two more that are not geometry at all and should not be confused with primitives:

- **Decal / lettering layer** — draft marks, name, port of registry, fishing PLN, load line, IMO
  symbols, stencilled service labels, escape route striping. ~14 rows. Player-authored text on the
  hull is the only part of a vessel that is *theirs* by authorship rather than assembly, which
  makes it disproportionately valuable for what it costs.
- **Paint regions with curved boundaries** — already identified in `REFERENCES.md` and confirmed
  many times over here: boot top, anti-fouling, swept colour flashes, funnel bands, non-slip
  coating panels, deck edge striping, weathering. Once colour is per-vertex this is free, and it is
  a very large fraction of a vessel's identity.

### 6. Baking has a compliance problem

`general_vessel` contains `white_above_sidelights` — a rule that compares **the height of one
placed item against the height of two others**. `brick_side` compares an item's position against
the vessel centreline. `mooring` counts items by tag. `passenger_capacity` sums an attribute across
seats.

None of these can be answered from a merged skin. Which means the live/baked distinction is not
only about interactivity:

> **An item must stay live if the player operates it, if it emits light, if it moves — or if any
> rule reads its position, its side, its height, or an attribute it carries.**

That fourth clause adds nav lights, mooring fittings, seats, helm consoles, fishing gear and cranes
to the live set on *compliance* grounds even where they never animate. 222 of 344 rows are live in
this dataset, and that ratio is not a failure of discipline — it is what a rules-driven vessel
costs. The mitigation is that a live node can still contribute a **baked mesh** while keeping a
lightweight transform and attribute record; live and baked need to be separable per part, not a
single flag on the whole item.

### 7. Compliance gaps this dataset surfaced

Not primitive problems, but they came out of the same work and are worth passing on:

- **`budget_caps.crane` is `0` for every registration** in `resources/data/vessels/registrations/catalog.json`, and both `cargo_vessel` and `bulk_vessel` carry a `crane_rating` rule with `max_rating: 0`. As written, **fitting any crane fails compliance on every licence in the game** — including the vessel class R3 is built around. Either a crane licence is missing or those caps are placeholders.
- **There is no lifesaving rule of any kind.** No liferaft count, no lifebuoy count, no muster station, no EPIRB. A `passenger_vessel` can be certified with four seats, a cabin and a door, and nothing to survive in. That is the largest hole in the rules vocabulary.
- **`fishing_vessel` requires fishing gear but not fishing lights.** Green-over-white is what tells another vessel you are dragging a trawl; it is the most teachable rule in the entire collision regulations and it is not represented.
- **A combined bicolour lantern** (legal under 20 m) must satisfy both `light_nav_port` and `light_nav_stbd`. If rules count brick ids, one item has to count as two — a real edge case for whoever implements it.
- **`exposed_deck_cells`, `cargo_cells` and the physical deck are three views of the same thing.** Aligning the lashing-eye grid, the cargo cell grid and the deck cell grid would make the rules legible from the deck itself, without any UI.

### The honest summary

The five primitives are **not insufficient — they are under-generalised.** Four changes carry
almost all of the gap:

1. **Sheer band → swept profile along a 3D polyline** (a dozen parts, one change)
2. **Spar → polyline with taper** (pipes, bent vents, curved davits, wrapped handrails)
3. **Wire → sag range including exactly zero, plus an honest admission that chain is unsolved**
4. **A grating/louvre material**, which is a shader rather than a primitive

After those, what genuinely remains missing is: compound curvature (hard, and possibly worth
deliberately scoping out), cloth and nets, a torus, and a **library of small fixed hardware meshes
placed with float coordinates** — which is not a primitive at all but a placement model.

And underneath all of it, the finding that outranks every primitive question: **half this catalog
is smaller than one grid cell.** Until `items[]` can be placed off-grid, most of the parts on this
list cannot exist at any size that is not wrong.
